# Unit 7: Introduction to Operating Systems – Reflective Models Activity

## Overview

This activity forms part of Unit 7 of my ePortfolio. It explores three models of reflection, examining the strengths and weaknesses of each before identifying the model considered most suitable for use in a postgraduate computing context. Reflection is a structured process that supports deeper and more meaningful learning by encouraging deliberate analysis of experience rather than passive recall.

The brief identifies three models from the provided reading — Boud's Triangular Representation (1985), Gibbs' Reflective Cycle (1988), and Atkins and Murphy (1993) — as a starting point. As directed, one of these is selected for analysis alongside two further models identified through independent research.

## The Three Models Explored

### 1. Gibbs' Reflective Cycle (1988)

Gibbs' Reflective Cycle was introduced in *Learning by Doing: A Guide to Teaching and Learning Methods* (Gibbs, 1988). It is built on six sequential stages: **Description** (what happened?), **Feelings** (what were you thinking and feeling?), **Evaluation** (what was good and bad about the experience?), **Analysis** (what sense can you make of the situation?), **Conclusion** (what else could you have done?), and **Action Plan** (if it arose again, what would you do?).

The cycle draws from Kolb's (1984) experiential learning theory, extending its ideas into a more detailed, question-driven format specifically designed to support structured written reflection.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** | The six-stage structure provides clear, sequential prompts that guide the reflective writer step by step, making it accessible to those who are new to formal reflection. The inclusion of feelings as an explicit stage distinguishes it from purely cognitive models and encourages honest self-assessment. Its cyclical format makes the connection between experience and future action explicit, reinforcing the idea that learning should lead to changed behaviour rather than simply recording what occurred. It is one of the most widely used and well-evidenced models in academic and professional contexts. |
| **Weaknesses** | The linear, stage-by-stage format can feel mechanical if followed rigidly, potentially producing formulaic reflective writing rather than genuine critical analysis. Gibbs' model does not explicitly prompt the writer to consider underpinning theory, professional frameworks, or literature, which can limit the depth of academic reflection at postgraduate level. The model was developed primarily for healthcare and teaching contexts, and some of its prompts (particularly around feelings) translate less naturally to technical computing disciplines. |

---

### 2. Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle (1984)

Kolb's Experiential Learning Theory (ELT), published in *Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development* (Kolb, 1984), proposes a four-stage cycle comprising: **Concrete Experience** (doing or having an experience), **Reflective Observation** (reviewing and reflecting on the experience), **Abstract Conceptualisation** (concluding and learning from the experience), and **Active Experimentation** (planning and trying out what has been learned). Kolb presents learning as a continuous, cyclical process where knowledge is created through the transformation of experience, and notes that entry to the cycle can occur at any stage.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** | Kolb's model is theoretically grounded and widely validated across disciplines. Its emphasis on active experimentation — moving from reflection back into action — makes it particularly relevant to technical learning, where the ability to test and apply new understanding in practice (for example, configuring a system, running a lab, or debugging code) is central. The cycle can be entered at any stage, which reflects the non-linear reality of how learning often happens in practice. It also provides the theoretical foundation upon which several subsequent models (including Gibbs') are built. |
| **Weaknesses** | Kolb's cycle has been criticised for being overly linear and simplistic, not fully capturing the complex, non-sequential nature of real-world learning (Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle, 2025). The model's accompanying Learning Style Inventory — which categorises learners into four types (Divergers, Assimilators, Convergers, Accommodators) — has been questioned as overly rigid, since individuals may not consistently exhibit a single style across different contexts. Additionally, several studies have found that in practice, the Concrete Experience and Active Experimentation stages are the least consistently applied, with practitioners substituting simulations or case studies in their place, which may weaken the model's outcomes (ScienceDirect, 2025). |

---

### 3. Schön's Reflective Model (1983)

Donald Schön introduced his model of reflective practice in *The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action* (Schön, 1983). Unlike stage-based models, Schön's approach is built on two complementary concepts: **Reflection-in-Action**, which involves thinking and adjusting in real-time as a situation unfolds, and **Reflection-on-Action**, which involves retrospective analysis of an experience after it has concluded. Schön developed the model as a direct critique of the dominant model of technical rationality in professional education, which separated research from practice and prioritised theoretical knowledge over experiential learning.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** | Schön's distinction between in-action and on-action reflection is uniquely valuable in fast-moving technical environments. Reflection-in-action captures the kind of real-time, adaptive thinking that is fundamental to live problem-solving — for example, adjusting an approach mid-way through a penetration test, or revising a system configuration as an unexpected error emerges. This makes it one of the most practically relevant models for computing professionals. Reflection-on-action, meanwhile, supports the kind of structured post-event analysis that sits naturally alongside documentation, after-action reviews, and ePortfolio entries. The model does not impose a rigid sequence, which allows it to accommodate the complexity of real professional situations more honestly than linear models. |
| **Weaknesses** | The term 'reflection-in-action' has been critiqued for lacking clarity: Court (1988, cited in Purdue e-Pubs) observed that Schön's examples often appear to involve removing oneself from the action in order to reflect, calling into question whether genuine in-action reflection is consistently distinct from brief pauses. The model has also been noted to have a self-protective individualistic orientation, with limited encouragement for the practitioner to question their own values or consider the ethical dimensions of their actions (Convery, 1998, cited in ResearchGate, 2020). Unlike Gibbs or Kolb, Schön provides no structured prompts or stages, which can make it less accessible for those new to formal reflective practice and difficult to use as the basis for structured written reflection required in academic assessments. |

---

## Optimum Model: Schön's Reflective Model (1983)

Having considered all three models, **Schön's Reflective Model** is the most appropriate choice for reflection in the context of postgraduate computing study, and specifically for the kind of learning that takes place in this module.

The primary reason for this choice is the distinction between reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action. The learning in this module regularly involves practical, technical activities — configuring systems, writing and testing code, debugging, and working through cybersecurity labs — where the ability to think and adapt in real-time is as important as retrospective analysis. Schön's model is the only one of the three examined here that explicitly names and values this in-the-moment thinking, making it a more accurate and honest account of how technical learning actually occurs.

Gibbs' cycle and Kolb's cycle are both useful and well-evidenced, but they impose a structured, sequential process that works well for reflecting on completed experiences with clear outcomes. In technical computing practice, experiences are often non-linear: a debugging session may loop back on itself, a lab exercise may reveal an unexpected gap in knowledge mid-way through, or a solution that appeared correct may need to be revised as new information emerges. Schön's model accommodates this more naturally.

At postgraduate level, the goal of reflection is not simply to describe what happened and how it felt (as Gibbs prompts), but to develop genuine critical awareness of one's own practice and to identify how that practice can be improved. Schön's reflection-on-action, used as the basis for ePortfolio entries, supports this kind of structured self-examination without the risk of producing formulaic, stage-by-stage responses.

The acknowledged limitation — the lack of structured prompts — is mitigated in an academic ePortfolio context by the clear expectations set by the module brief itself, which provides sufficient scaffolding. The absence of a rigid template is, in this context, an advantage rather than a drawback.
